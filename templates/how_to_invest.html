How to Invest

A practical framework for choosing stronger companies, managing risk, and knowing why you own a stock.

01

Start With the Business, Not the Stock Price

A stock is not just a line moving up and down on a chart. A stock represents ownership in a business. Before buying, the first question should be simple:

Does this company deserve to be owned?

A falling stock price does not automatically make something cheap, and a rising stock price does not automatically make something good. The business comes first. The stock price comes second.

02

Look for Business Quality

Strong companies usually have the financial power to survive weak markets and benefit when conditions improve. Business quality is about how strong the company itself is.

Revenue Growth

Is the company selling more over time?

Profit Growth

Is the company turning sales into higher earnings?

Margins

Does the company keep a healthy amount of profit from each dollar of revenue?

Free Cash Flow

Does the business generate real cash after paying for operations and major investments?

Debt

Can the company handle what it owes?

Competitive Position

Does the company have advantages that competitors cannot easily copy?

03

Look for Opportunity

A great company is not always a great investment. If the price is too high, the future upside may already be priced in. A weaker company is not automatically bad either, especially if a turnaround is realistic.

The strongest setups usually combine a good business with a clear reason the stock could become more valuable.

Common sources of opportunity

  • Industry tailwinds, such as AI, cloud infrastructure, defense spending, energy demand, or manufacturing expansion.
  • Company-specific catalysts, such as new products, margin improvement, restructuring, or major contracts.
  • Market misunderstanding, where investors may be underestimating the company’s future earnings power.
  • Reasonable valuation, where the stock price still leaves room for upside.
  • Improving financial results, especially when revenue, margins, and cash flow move in the right direction together.
04

Know Why the Stock Could Rise

Before buying a stock, you should be able to explain what could make the company more valuable.

Weak reason

“I think it will go up.”

Better reason

“Revenue is growing, margins are improving, demand is rising, and the market may be undervaluing the company.”

05

Manage Risk Before Buying

Risk management is not something to think about after the stock drops. It should be part of the decision before buying.

Do not buy just because of hype.Popularity is not the same as quality.
Know the biggest risk.Every investment has something that could go wrong.
Watch debt.High debt can turn a normal slowdown into a serious problem.
Respect valuation.A great company can still be a bad investment if the price is too high.
Diversify.Do not let one stock control your entire outcome.
Know what would change your mind.If the facts change, the thesis should change too.
06

Know When to Buy or Sell

When buying makes more sense

Buying makes more sense when the business case is strong, the opportunity is clear, and the price still leaves room for upside.

  • The company has solid or improving fundamentals.
  • There is a clear catalyst or long-term tailwind.
  • The valuation is reasonable compared with the company’s future potential.
  • The risks are understood, not ignored.
  • You can clearly explain why you own it.

When selling makes more sense

  • The original thesis breaks.
  • Growth slows in a way that changes the long-term story.
  • Margins weaken and do not recover.
  • Debt becomes dangerous.
  • The stock becomes extremely overvalued.
  • A better opportunity exists elsewhere.
  • You no longer understand why you own it.
07

Terms Glossary

These are the terms investors should understand before reading a company report or making a decision.

Basic investing terms

Stock

Ownership in a company. If the company becomes more valuable, the stock may rise. If the company weakens, the stock may fall.

Share

One unit of ownership in a company.

Ticker

The short symbol used to identify a stock, such as AAPL for Apple or NVDA for Nvidia.

Bond

A loan made to a company or government. Bond investors usually receive interest payments and expect repayment at maturity.

ETF

An exchange-traded fund. It holds a basket of investments and trades like a stock.

Index Fund

A fund designed to track a market index, such as the S&P 500.

Portfolio

The full collection of investments someone owns.

Diversification

Spreading money across different investments to reduce dependence on one company or sector.

Company and financial terms

Revenue

The money a company brings in from selling products or services.

Profit / Net Income

What remains after expenses, taxes, interest, and other costs.

Earnings

Another word for profit, often used when discussing public companies.

EPS

Earnings per share. It shows how much profit is assigned to each share.

Gross Margin

The percentage of revenue left after the direct cost of producing goods or services.

Profit Margin

The percentage of revenue that becomes profit after expenses.

Free Cash Flow

Cash the company generates after paying for operations and capital investments.

Debt

Money the company owes. Debt is not always bad, but too much debt raises risk.

Return on Equity

A measure of how efficiently a company generates profit from shareholder capital.

Balance Sheet

A financial statement showing assets, liabilities, and shareholder equity.

Valuation terms

Valuation

How expensive or cheap a stock looks compared with the company’s earnings, growth, cash flow, or assets.

Market Cap

The total value of a company’s shares. It equals share price multiplied by shares outstanding.

P/E Ratio

Price-to-earnings ratio. It compares the stock price with the company’s earnings per share.

Forward P/E

A P/E ratio based on expected future earnings instead of past earnings.

PEG Ratio

A valuation metric that compares the P/E ratio with expected earnings growth.

Multiple

A shortcut investors use to describe valuation, such as “20 times earnings.”

Undervalued

When a stock may trade below what the business is reasonably worth.

Overvalued

When a stock may trade above what the business is reasonably worth.

Risk and market terms

Risk

The chance that an investment performs worse than expected or permanently loses value.

Volatility

How much a stock price moves up and down.

Liquidity

How easily an investment can be bought or sold without major price movement.

Beta

A measure of how volatile a stock is compared with the broader market.

Drawdown

A decline from a previous high.

Margin of Safety

A cushion between the price paid and what the investor believes the business is worth.

Catalyst

An event or development that could cause investors to value the company differently.

Tailwind

A trend that helps a company or industry grow.

Headwind

A trend or challenge that makes growth harder.

Thesis

The clear reason for owning an investment.

Thesis report terms

Business Quality Rating

A score focused on the strength of the company itself.

Opportunity Rating

A relative-valuation score based on annual earnings, free-cash-flow and EBITDA yields against disclosed peers. Analyst targets and momentum are context only. Company catalysts need separate review.

Meets our research screen?

A decision rule based on the combined score and minimum quality threshold.

Bull Case

The argument for why the stock could perform well.

Bear Case

The argument for why the stock could perform poorly.

What We’re Watching

The key facts or trends that could strengthen or weaken the investment case.

08

Final Rule

If you cannot clearly explain why you own a stock, you probably should not own it.
